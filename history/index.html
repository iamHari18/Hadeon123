Part 1: History

The eras below overlap: vacuum tubes were still common long after the transistor arrived, and wireless grew alongside chips. Dates are the year an invention was first shown or published, unless noted.

Foundations 1800–1900

Before anything could be called electronics, people had to learn what electricity is, how it relates to magnetism, and that it can travel as waves.

  • 1800

    Volta’s battery

    Alessandro Volta stacks zinc and copper discs with brine-soaked cloth to make the voltaic pile.

    Why it mattered: The first steady source of current. Experiments with circuits, rather than sparks, begin here.

    Connects to: Network theory: ideal and practical voltage sources.

  • 1831

    Faraday discovers electromagnetic induction

    Michael Faraday shows that a changing magnetic field induces a voltage in a nearby coil.

    Why it mattered: The basis of generators, transformers and inductors, and of the electricity grid.

    Connects to: Electromagnetics: Faraday’s law; network theory: inductors and coupled coils.

  • 1865

    Maxwell’s equations

    James Clerk Maxwell publishes “A Dynamical Theory of the Electromagnetic Field”, unifying electricity, magnetism and light.

    Why it mattered: Predicts electromagnetic waves travelling at the speed of light, the theory behind every antenna and radio link.

    Connects to: Electromagnetics and antenna theory.

  • 1874

    First semiconductor effect: rectification

    Ferdinand Braun finds that a metal point pressed on certain crystals (metal sulphides) conducts better in one direction than the other.

    Why it mattered: The first rectifier made from a solid material, and the ancestor of the crystal radio detector and the diode.

  • 1883

    The Edison effect

    Thomas Edison notices that current flows in a vacuum bulb from the hot filament to a separate metal plate, but only one way.

    Why it mattered: This is thermionic emission. Twenty years later it became the working principle of the vacuum tube.

  • 1887

    Hertz proves radio waves exist

    Heinrich Hertz generates and detects electromagnetic waves in his lab with a spark gap and a loop receiver.

    Why it mattered: Confirms Maxwell’s prediction. The unit of frequency is named after him.

  • 1895

    J. C. Bose and millimetre waves

    In Calcutta, Jagadish Chandra Bose demonstrates wireless signalling at wavelengths down to 5 mm (about 60 GHz), using horn antennas, dielectric lenses and a coherer detector.

    Why it mattered: Decades ahead of its time; IEEE named it a Milestone in 2012. The same band is now used by 5G and automotive radar.

    Connects to: Microwave engineering: waveguides and horn antennas.

  • 1897

    The electron, and the cathode-ray tube

    J. J. Thomson identifies the electron as a particle. In the same year Ferdinand Braun builds the first cathode-ray tube.

    Why it mattered: Electronics is literally the control of electrons. The CRT became the screen of oscilloscopes and televisions for most of the 20th century.

Vacuum tube era 1900–1947

Glass tubes that controlled electrons in a vacuum gave the world radio, long-distance telephony and the first electronic computers.

  • 1901

    Radio crosses the Atlantic

    Guglielmo Marconi reports receiving the Morse letter “S” in Newfoundland, sent from Cornwall, England.

    Why it mattered: Shows that radio can work over thousands of kilometres, and starts wireless as an industry.

  • 1904

    Fleming valve, the first vacuum tube

    John Ambrose Fleming patents a two-electrode vacuum tube, a diode, to detect radio signals.

    Why it mattered: The first practical electronic device: current flows one way through a vacuum.

    Connects to: Analog circuits: rectifiers.

  • 1906

    De Forest’s Audion (triode)

    Lee de Forest adds a control grid between filament and plate.

    Why it mattered: The first device that could amplify a signal. Amplification made radio broadcasting and transcontinental telephone lines possible.

    Connects to: Analog circuits: amplifiers and transconductance.

  • 1918

    The superheterodyne receiver

    Edwin Armstrong invents the superheterodyne receiver, which shifts every incoming station to one fixed intermediate frequency (IF).

    Why it mattered: Almost every radio receiver since, including the one in your phone, uses this idea in some form.

    Connects to: Communication systems: mixers and IF stages.

  • 1927

    The negative feedback amplifier

    Harold Black at Bell Labs works out that feeding a fraction of the output back, inverted, makes an amplifier stable and low-distortion.

    Why it mattered: It gave up some gain in exchange for accuracy and stability. Every op-amp circuit and control loop relies on it.

    Connects to: LIC and control systems: feedback, gain and stability.

  • 1945

    ENIAC

    ENIAC, a general-purpose electronic computer with about 17,468 vacuum tubes, is completed in the US and shown to the public in February 1946.

    Why it mattered: It showed that electronic computing worked, but also how hot, big and unreliable tubes were. The industry needed something better.

    Connects to: Digital electronics: logic built from switches.

Transistor era 1947–1958

A tiny solid-state switch replaced the hot, fragile vacuum tube, and an information theory arrived at the same time.

  • Dec 1947

    The point-contact transistor

    John Bardeen and Walter Brattain, in William Shockley’s group at Bell Labs, build the first transistor from germanium. Shockley then devises the junction transistor (1948), and working junction transistors are announced in 1951.

    Why it mattered: The most important invention in electronics. The three shared the 1956 Nobel Prize in Physics.

    Connects to: Solid state devices: BJT operation.

  • 1948

    Shannon’s information theory

    Claude Shannon publishes “A Mathematical Theory of Communication”.

    Why it mattered: Defines information in bits and the maximum data rate of a noisy channel. Every modem and wireless standard is measured against this limit.

    Connects to: Communication: channel capacity, C = B log₂(1 + S/N).

  • 1954

    The silicon transistor

    Gordon Teal’s team at Texas Instruments announces the first commercial silicon transistor.

    Why it mattered: Silicon works at higher temperatures than germanium and grows a stable oxide. It became the material of the whole industry.

    Connects to: Solid state devices: bandgap and temperature.

  • 1957

    Fairchild Semiconductor is founded

    Eight engineers (the “traitorous eight”) leave Shockley Semiconductor and start Fairchild in California.

    Why it mattered: Fairchild developed the planar process and the silicon IC, and its people went on to start Intel, AMD and many others.

Integrated circuit era 1958–1971

Many transistors on one piece of silicon: the step that made electronics cheap and made Moore’s law possible.

  • 1958

    Kilby’s integrated circuit

    Jack Kilby at Texas Instruments demonstrates a working circuit on a single piece of germanium on 12 September.

    Why it mattered: Proves that resistors, capacitors and transistors can be made from one block of semiconductor. Kilby received the Nobel Prize in 2000.

  • 1959

    The planar process and Noyce’s silicon IC

    Jean Hoerni at Fairchild invents the planar process; Robert Noyce uses it to design a monolithic silicon IC with its wiring laid on top.

    Why it mattered: This is how chips are still made: layers built up on a flat, oxide-protected silicon wafer.

    Connects to: VLSI: fabrication steps (oxidation, photolithography, diffusion, metallisation).

  • 1959–60

    The MOSFET

    Mohamed Atalla and Dawon Kahng at Bell Labs make the first working metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistor.

    Why it mattered: The MOSFET is simple to make and to shrink. Today it is by far the most manufactured device in history.

    Connects to: Solid state devices: MOS capacitor and MOSFET.

  • 1963

    CMOS

    Frank Wanlass at Fairchild invents complementary MOS logic, pairing an n-channel and a p-channel transistor.

    Why it mattered: A CMOS gate uses almost no power when it is not switching. Nearly all digital chips use CMOS.

    Connects to: Digital electronics and VLSI: CMOS inverter.

  • 1965

    Moore’s law

    Gordon Moore observes that the number of components on a chip is doubling every year. In 1975 he revises the rate to every two years.

    Why it mattered: Became the industry’s roadmap for about 50 years: planned, funded and met by thousands of engineers.

  • 1966–70

    One-transistor DRAM

    Robert Dennard at IBM invents the one-transistor, one-capacitor memory cell in 1966. Intel’s 1103 (1970) becomes the first commercially successful DRAM chip.

    Why it mattered: Cheap, dense memory replaced magnetic core memory. The same 1T1C cell is still the basis of DRAM.

    Connects to: Digital electronics: memories.

  • 1971

    Intel 4004, the first commercial microprocessor

    Intel announces the 4004: a 4-bit CPU with about 2,300 transistors on a 10 µm process, designed for a Busicom calculator.

    Why it mattered: A whole processor on one chip. From here, computing became something you could put in any product.

    Connects to: Microprocessors and embedded systems.

Microprocessor and personal computing era 1972–1990

Design tools, scaling rules and new business models made it possible to build ever larger chips and sell computers to everyone.

  • 1973

    SPICE circuit simulator

    SPICE is released at the University of California, Berkeley.

    Why it mattered: Engineers could simulate a circuit before building it. Almost every analog simulator today, including the free ones you use, descends from SPICE.

    Connects to: Analog circuits and LIC labs: simulation.

  • 1974

    Dennard scaling

    Robert Dennard and colleagues publish the rules for shrinking MOSFETs: scale dimensions and voltage together and power density stays constant.

    Why it mattered: Explained why smaller transistors were faster and more efficient at once. When voltage could no longer be scaled (in the mid-2000s), clock speeds stopped rising and multicore chips took over.

    Connects to: VLSI: scaling and short-channel effects.

  • 1981

    The IBM PC

    IBM launches its Personal Computer using Intel’s 8088, a version of the 16-bit 8086 (1978).

    Why it mattered: The x86 architecture became the standard for PCs and, later, servers.

    Connects to: Microprocessors: 8086 architecture.

  • 1984–87

    HDLs and the FPGA

    Verilog is created (1984), Xilinx ships the first commercial FPGA, the XC2064 (1985), and VHDL becomes IEEE standard 1076 (1987).

    Why it mattered: Hardware began to be designed by writing code, and could be reconfigured after manufacturing.

    Connects to: Digital design and Verilog labs.

  • 1985

    The first ARM processor

    Acorn Computers in Britain powers up ARM1, a simple low-power RISC processor designed by a team including Sophie Wilson and Steve Furber.

    Why it mattered: Its low power is why ARM-based chips now run nearly every smartphone.

    Connects to: Computer architecture: RISC versus CISC.

  • 1987

    TSMC and the foundry model

    Morris Chang founds Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company, the first dedicated contract chipmaker.

    Why it mattered: Companies could design chips without owning a fab (the fabless model). This is how Nvidia, Qualcomm and many Indian design startups work.

Mobile and wireless era 1973–2019

These overlap with the eras above: while chips shrank, radio moved from the car and the office into everyone’s pocket.

  • 1973

    First handheld mobile phone call

    Martin Cooper of Motorola makes a call on a DynaTAC prototype in New York. The first commercial cellular network (1G, analog) opens in Tokyo in 1979.

    Why it mattered: Shows that a phone can belong to a person, not a place.

  • 1991

    The first GSM call (2G)

    The first call on a GSM network is made in Finland on 1 July 1991.

    Why it mattered: Digital voice meant better capacity, encryption and SMS. India’s first mobile call, in 1995, was on GSM.

    Connects to: Digital communication: TDMA and GMSK.

  • 1997

    Wi-Fi: IEEE 802.11

    The first IEEE 802.11 wireless LAN standard is published, running at up to 2 Mbit/s.

    Why it mattered: Wireless networking for homes and offices. Later versions added OFDM and MIMO to reach gigabit speeds.

    Connects to: Communication: OFDM.

  • 2007

    The iPhone

    Apple launches the iPhone, combining a phone, computer and touchscreen around an ARM-based processor.

    Why it mattered: Smartphones became the largest market for chips, displays, sensors and RF front-ends.

    Connects to: Embedded systems and sensors.

  • 2009

    4G LTE

    TeliaSonera opens the first commercial LTE networks, in Stockholm and Oslo, in December 2009.

    Why it mattered: An all-IP network built on OFDMA and MIMO made mobile video and apps practical.

    Connects to: Communication: OFDMA and MIMO.

  • 2019

    5G

    The first commercial 5G smartphone services open in South Korea and the US in April 2019. India launches 5G on 1 October 2022.

    Why it mattered: Adds wider bandwidths, massive MIMO antennas and millimetre-wave bands, the band J. C. Bose experimented in.

    Connects to: Antennas and microwave engineering.

Modern era 2007–today

Shrinking alone is no longer enough. Progress now comes from new transistor shapes, new lithography, packaging several dies together, and chips built for AI.

  • 2007

    High-k metal gate

    Intel’s 45 nm process replaces the silicon dioxide gate insulator with a hafnium-based “high-k” material and a metal gate.

    Why it mattered: The SiO₂ layer had become only a few atoms thick and leaked too much. High-k kept gate control strong without the leakage.

    Connects to: Solid state devices: gate oxide capacitance.

  • 2011

    FinFET goes into production

    Intel announces its 22 nm “Tri-Gate” FinFET process, shipped in 2012. Other foundries follow at 16/14 nm.

    Why it mattered: The gate wraps three sides of a raised fin, controlling leakage much better than a flat transistor.

    Connects to: VLSI: short-channel effects.

  • 2016

    Chips built for AI

    Google reveals its Tensor Processing Unit (TPU), in use in its data centres since 2015. Nvidia adds Tensor Cores to its Volta GPUs in 2017.

    Why it mattered: Neural networks are mostly matrix multiplication, so dedicated hardware for it is far more efficient than a general CPU.

    Connects to: Digital design: multiply-accumulate (MAC) arrays.

  • 2018–19

    EUV lithography in volume production

    Extreme-ultraviolet scanners from ASML, using 13.5 nm light, enter high-volume manufacturing at Samsung and TSMC on 7 nm-class processes.

    Why it mattered: Allows finer patterns with fewer exposure steps. Every leading-edge chip now depends on it.

  • 2019

    Chiplets go mainstream

    AMD’s Zen 2 processors combine several small 7 nm CPU dies with a separate I/O die in one package. In 2022 the UCIe consortium is formed to standardise die-to-die links.

    Why it mattered: Small dies have better yield, and each part can use the process that suits it best.

  • 2022

    Gate-all-around transistors

    Samsung starts 3 nm production with gate-all-around (GAA) transistors, the first company to put them into production.

    Why it mattered: The gate surrounds stacked silicon sheets on all four sides, giving the best control yet over leakage.

    Connects to: Solid state devices: electrostatics of the channel.

  • 2025–26

    2 nm-class nanosheets and backside power

    TSMC begins volume production of its 2 nm (N2) nanosheet process in the fourth quarter of 2025. Intel launches Panther Lake on its 18A process in January 2026, the first high-volume chips with backside power delivery (PowerVia).

    Why it mattered: The industry’s leading edge is now built on GAA transistors, with power wiring moving to the back of the wafer.

India in electronics

India’s chip story has had a strong start, a long pause, and a big restart. Here are the key points, from research labs to the first commercial fab.

What this means for you

The new plants need process, equipment, test and packaging engineers, not only chip designers. Device physics (Solid State Devices), VLSI fabrication and analog circuits are directly useful for these jobs. Follow new announcements on the news page.

Part 2: The future

These are the directions most likely to shape the next ten years of electronics. Each one says, in plain words, what it is, where it really stands today, and what to learn if it interests you. The labels show how far along each one is:

Shipping now in real productsResearch working in labs or pilots, not yet in mass use

Forecast dates come from the companies or standards bodies named, and they often slip. Where a date is a plan rather than a fact, the text says so.

Gate-all-around and CFET transistors In production (GAA) · Research (CFET)

In plain words
A gate-all-around (GAA) transistor stacks thin horizontal silicon sheets and wraps the gate around every one of them, so the gate controls the channel from all sides. A CFET (complementary FET) goes one step further and stacks the n-type transistor directly on top of the p-type one, roughly halving the area of a logic cell.
Where it stands
GAA nanosheets are in volume production: Samsung since 2022, TSMC N2 since late 2025 and Intel 18A since 2025–26. CFET is still in research; imec expects it to enter the roadmap around its “A7” node, after several more generations of nanosheets.
Skills to build
Device physics (electrostatics, short-channel effects), TCAD simulation, process integration, and standard-cell design.

Backside power delivery Shipping now

In plain words
In a normal chip, power wires and signal wires compete for space in the metal layers on top of the transistors. Backside power moves the power network to the underside of the thinned wafer, so power arrives with less voltage drop and signals get more room.
Where it stands
Intel’s 18A process with PowerVia is shipping in Panther Lake laptops (launched January 2026). TSMC’s first backside-power node, A16, was expected in 2026; in April 2026 TSMC said volume production would begin in 2027.
Skills to build
Power integrity (IR drop analysis), physical design and place-and-route, thermal analysis.

Chiplets and advanced packaging Shipping now, growing fast

In plain words
Instead of one huge die, a product is built from several smaller dies (chiplets) placed side by side or stacked, and connected with very dense wiring inside the package. High-bandwidth memory (HBM) stacked next to an AI processor is the best-known example.
Where it stands
Mainstream in CPUs and AI accelerators. The UCIe standard for die-to-die links was launched in March 2022 by companies including Intel, AMD, Arm, TSMC and Samsung. Packaging capacity is now one of the main limits on AI chip supply. India’s new ATMP/OSAT plants work in this part of the industry.
Skills to build
Signal integrity, high-speed SerDes and die-to-die interfaces, package design, thermal and mechanical analysis, test.

Silicon photonics and co-packaged optics Early products

In plain words
Silicon photonics builds optical parts (waveguides, modulators, photodetectors) on silicon wafers using chip-making tools. Co-packaged optics puts these optical engines right next to a switch chip, so data leaves the package as light instead of travelling over long copper traces.
Where it stands
Optical transceivers using silicon photonics are already common in data centres. In March 2025 Nvidia announced network switches with co-packaged optics: the InfiniBand version for late 2025 and the Ethernet version for 2026. Nvidia claims about 3.5× better power efficiency; independent figures are still limited.
Skills to build
Optical communication, electromagnetics, analog and mixed-signal design (drivers, transimpedance amplifiers), and photonic layout.

Wide-bandgap power: SiC and GaN Shipping now

In plain words
Silicon carbide (bandgap about 3.3 eV) and gallium nitride (about 3.4 eV) can block much higher voltages than silicon (1.12 eV) in a smaller device, and switch faster with lower losses.
Where it stands
SiC MOSFETs are used in electric-vehicle traction inverters (Tesla’s Model 3 was an early high-volume example) and in solar inverters; GaN is used in compact phone and laptop chargers and in RF amplifiers for base stations. India’s 2021 semiconductor programme includes support for compound-semiconductor fabs, the category SiC and GaN belong to.
Skills to build
Power electronics (converters, gate drivers), device physics, magnetics, thermal design, and EMI.

Neuromorphic and in-memory computing Research and pilot systems

In plain words
Most of the energy in AI hardware goes into moving data between memory and the processor. In-memory computing does the multiply-add inside the memory array itself (for example, by summing currents through rows of resistive cells). Neuromorphic chips imitate spiking neurons that only use energy when an event happens.
Where it stands
Mostly research. Intel’s Hala Point system (April 2024) at Sandia National Laboratories links 1,152 Loihi 2 chips with about 1.15 billion artificial neurons. No neuromorphic chip is yet in mass-market use; analog in-memory computing is still in prototypes.
Skills to build
Analog and mixed-signal design, memory devices (SRAM, RRAM, memristors), computer architecture, and basic machine learning.

Quantum computing hardware Research

In plain words
A quantum computer stores information in qubits that can be in a superposition of 0 and 1. Superconducting qubits, the type used by Google and IBM, are tiny circuits cooled to around a hundredth of a degree above absolute zero and controlled with microwave pulses.
Where it stands
In December 2024 Google’s 105-qubit Willow chip showed error correction getting better as more qubits were added (“below threshold”), a key step. Useful, large-scale fault-tolerant machines do not exist yet and need far more qubits.
Skills to build
Microwave engineering, RF measurement, cryogenic electronics, control systems, low-noise amplifiers, and FPGA-based control hardware.

6G and terahertz communication Standards being written

In plain words
6G is the next generation of mobile networks. Ideas being studied include new mid-band spectrum, AI built into the network, joint sensing and communication, and, in the longer term, sub-terahertz frequencies for very short, very fast links.
Where it stands
In 3GPP, the 6G study (Release 20) started in mid-2025. The specifications themselves (Release 21) are planned from 2027 to the end of 2028, with the first 6G specifications expected in early 2029 and commercial systems expected around 2030. Sub-THz links are still in research.
Skills to build
Communication theory, MIMO and signal processing, antenna and RF design, and simulation in MATLAB or Python.

Flexible and printed electronics Niche products, active research

In plain words
Circuits printed or deposited on plastic, paper or fabric instead of rigid silicon wafers, using thin-film transistors and printable inks. They are slower than silicon chips but can bend, cover large areas, and cost very little per unit area.
Where it stands
Flexible OLED displays are already mainstream in foldable phones. Printed sensors, smart labels and wearable patches exist but are still a small market. Fully printed logic for everyday products remains mostly research.
Skills to build
Thin-film transistor physics, materials science, low-power analog design, and sensor interfacing.
The common thread

Almost every direction above rests on the same core subjects: solid state devices, analog and mixed-signal circuits, electromagnetics and signal processing. The study material and placement questions on this site cover those foundations.

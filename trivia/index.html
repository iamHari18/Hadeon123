Round 1: Inventions and inventors

Who built what, and when. Warm-up round.

1Who built the first electric battery, and in which year did he announce it?Easy

Answer: Alessandro Volta, with his “voltaic pile” of zinc and copper discs separated by brine-soaked cloth, announced in 1800.

Why it’s interesting: It was the first source of steady current. Before it, experimenters only had brief sparks from static electricity. The unit volt is named after him.

2Who discovered electromagnetic induction in 1831?Easy

Answer: Michael Faraday.

Why it’s interesting: A changing magnetic field creates a voltage. Every transformer, generator and inductor you study works on this one idea. The farad is named after him.

3In 1904 John Ambrose Fleming patented the first vacuum tube. What did his two-electrode “valve” do?Medium

Answer: It was a diode: current flowed in one direction only, so it rectified and detected radio signals.

Why it’s interesting: It was built on the “Edison effect” (1883), where current flows from a hot filament to a nearby plate in a vacuum. Electronics as a field starts here.

4What did Lee de Forest add to Fleming’s valve in 1906, and why did it matter?Medium

Answer: A third electrode, the grid, making the triode (he called it the Audion).

Why it’s interesting: A small voltage on the grid controls a big plate current. That is amplification, and it made long-distance telephony, radio broadcasting and early computers possible.

5Which three Bell Labs scientists shared the 1956 Nobel Prize in Physics for the transistor?Easy

Answer: John Bardeen, Walter Brattain and William Shockley.

Why it’s interesting: Bardeen and Brattain built the point-contact transistor in December 1947; Shockley, who led the group, then worked out the junction transistor. Bardeen later won a second physics Nobel, for superconductivity.

6Jack Kilby demonstrated the first integrated circuit on 12 September 1958. Which semiconductor was it made of?Medium

Answer: Germanium. Robert Noyce at Fairchild made a silicon version in 1959 using the planar process.

Why it’s interesting: Kilby’s chip had its parts joined by tiny hand-attached wires; Noyce’s design put the wiring on the chip itself, which is how every IC is made today. Kilby received the Nobel Prize in 2000.

7Who invented CMOS logic, and at which company?Hard

Answer: Frank Wanlass, at Fairchild Semiconductor, in 1963.

Why it’s interesting: A CMOS gate draws almost no current when it is not switching. That low static power is why almost every digital chip today, from microcontrollers to GPUs, uses CMOS.

8Who designed the 555 timer IC?Medium

Answer: Hans Camenzind, under contract to Signetics. He designed it in 1971 and Signetics put it on the market in 1972.

Why it’s interesting: It is often called the most popular integrated circuit ever made, and some estimates put production at over a billion a year. Astable, monostable, PWM: it is still in every lab kit.

Round 2: Components and laws

The rules behind every circuit you have ever solved.

9Georg Ohm published the law V = IR in which year?Easy

Answer: 1827.

Why it’s interesting: His work was not well received at first. Recognition came later: the Royal Society gave him its Copley Medal in 1841.

10Gustav Kirchhoff stated his current and voltage laws in 1845. What was he at the time?Medium

Answer: A university student, in Königsberg (now Kaliningrad).

Why it’s interesting: KCL is conservation of charge and KVL is conservation of energy. Nodal and mesh analysis, which you use in every network theory paper, are built on them.

11A resistor has bands brown, black, red, gold. What is its value?Easy

Answer: 1 kΩ ± 5%. Brown = 1, black = 0, red = multiplier × 100, gold = ±5% tolerance.

Why it’s interesting: The digit colours run black, brown, red, orange, yellow, green, blue, violet, grey, white for 0 to 9, and the middle stretch from red (2) to violet (7) is in rainbow order. A silver band means ±10%.

12What was the first capacitor, invented in 1745–46?Medium

Answer: The Leyden jar: a glass jar coated with metal foil inside and outside. Ewald von Kleist and Pieter van Musschenbroek invented it independently.

Why it’s interesting: Two conductors separated by an insulator is still the definition of a capacitor. The glass was the dielectric.

13Norton’s theorem is from 1926. Who published the Thévenin equivalent first, and roughly when?Hard

Answer: Hermann von Helmholtz, in 1853. Léon Charles Thévenin published it independently in 1883, and the name stuck to him.

Why it’s interesting: Edward Norton described the current-source version in a 1926 Bell Labs memo. Hans Ferdinand Mayer found the same result the same year, so in Germany it is sometimes called the Mayer–Norton theorem.

14Which circuit element did Leon Chua propose in 1971 as the “missing fourth” after R, L and C?Hard

Answer: The memristor: a resistor whose resistance depends on how much charge has passed through it.

Why it’s interesting: It stayed theoretical until 2008, when a team at HP Labs reported a titanium-dioxide device behaving like one. Memristor-like devices are now studied for in-memory and neuromorphic computing.

15The minus sign in Faraday’s law (e = −dΦ/dt) is named after which scientist?Easy

Answer: Heinrich Lenz (Lenz’s law, 1834).

Why it’s interesting: The induced current always opposes the change that caused it. That is why an inductor resists sudden changes in current, and why the back-EMF exists in motors.

16In 1879 Edwin Hall found a sideways voltage across a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field. Name one everyday use.Medium

Answer: Hall-effect sensors, for example to sense rotor position in brushless DC motors, measure current without contact, or detect a magnet (a laptop lid closing).

Why it’s interesting: The sign of the Hall voltage tells you whether the charge carriers are electrons or holes. It is how a lab measures whether a semiconductor sample is n-type or p-type.

Round 3: Semiconductors and chips

Device physics and fabs, without the derivations.

17Was the first transistor made of silicon or germanium?Easy

Answer: Germanium.

Why it’s interesting: Germanium was easier to purify in the 1940s. Silicon took over from the mid-1950s because it works at higher temperatures and grows an excellent insulating oxide (SiO₂).

18Adding phosphorus to silicon makes it n-type or p-type?Easy

Answer: n-type. Phosphorus has five valence electrons, one more than silicon, so it donates an electron. Boron (three) makes p-type.

Why it’s interesting: Every PN junction, BJT and MOSFET is built by placing these two kinds of doping side by side.

19What is the bandgap of silicon at room temperature, roughly?Medium

Answer: About 1.12 eV.

Why it’s interesting: Gallium nitride is about 3.4 eV and 4H silicon carbide about 3.3 eV. That wider gap lets them block much higher voltages, which is why they are used in EV and fast-charger power electronics.

20Who invented the planar process in 1959, and why was it a big deal?Hard

Answer: Jean Hoerni, at Fairchild Semiconductor.

Why it’s interesting: It builds devices into a flat silicon surface protected by a layer of oxide, so wiring can be laid on top. Noyce’s silicon IC used it, and chips are still made this way.

21Moore’s law: in his 1965 article, how often did Gordon Moore say the number of components per chip would double?Medium

Answer: Every year. In 1975 he revised it to every two years.

Why it’s interesting: The article was “Cramming more components onto integrated circuits”, in Electronics magazine. It was an observation about economics, not a law of physics.

22What wavelength of light does EUV lithography use, and how many companies make EUV scanners?Medium

Answer: 13.5 nm. One company makes them: ASML of the Netherlands.

Why it’s interesting: Older deep-UV tools use 193 nm light. EUV is absorbed by air and glass, so the whole light path is in vacuum and uses mirrors instead of lenses.

23Which company put FinFET (it called them “Tri-Gate”) transistors into volume production first, and at what node?Hard

Answer: Intel, at 22 nm. It announced the process in 2011 and shipped it in Ivy Bridge processors from 2012.

Why it’s interesting: Wrapping the gate around a raised fin of silicon on three sides gives much better control of leakage than a flat (planar) transistor.

24TSMC was founded in 1987 by Morris Chang. What new business model did it start?Medium

Answer: The pure-play foundry: a company that only manufactures chips designed by other companies.

Why it’s interesting: It made “fabless” companies possible, such as Nvidia, Qualcomm and MediaTek, which design chips but own no factory. Today AMD and Apple also rely on TSMC to make their chips.

Round 4: Famous ICs and processors

The chips that made history (and your lab manuals).

25How many transistors were in the Intel 4004, the first commercial microprocessor (1971)?Easy

Answer: About 2,300, made on a 10 µm process.

Why it’s interesting: It was designed for a Busicom printing calculator from Japan. Intel announced it in the 15 November 1971 issue of Electronic News.

26What does the “5” in Intel 8085 refer to?Medium

Answer: It needs only a single +5 V power supply. The earlier 8080 needed +5 V, −5 V and +12 V.

Why it’s interesting: One supply rail made boards simpler and cheaper, which is part of why the 8085 became a teaching favourite.

27In which year did Intel introduce the 8051 microcontroller family (MCS-51)?Easy

Answer: 1980.

Why it’s interesting: Over 45 years later, 8051-compatible cores are still built into many low-cost chips, and it is still in many university syllabi.

28The µA741 op-amp (Fairchild, 1968) had one feature that made it far easier to use than earlier op-amps. What was it?Hard

Answer: Internal frequency compensation: a 30 pF capacitor on the chip, so it was stable without external parts. It was designed by Dave Fullagar.

Why it’s interesting: Earlier op-amps such as Bob Widlar’s µA709 (1965) needed external compensation components. The 741 is often called the most popular op-amp of all time.

29When the first ARM chip was powered up on 26 April 1985, the ammeter read zero. Why?Hard

Answer: A fault on the test board meant the chip was not connected to its power supply. It was running on leakage current through its input pins.

Why it’s interesting: ARM1 used only about a tenth of a watt. That low-power design is why ARM went on to dominate phones. It was designed at Acorn by a team including Sophie Wilson and Steve Furber.

30The MOS 6502 sold for $25 in 1975, far cheaper than its rivals. Name one famous machine that used it (or a variant).Medium

Answer: The Apple II, the Commodore 64 (6510 variant) and the Nintendo Entertainment System (a Ricoh chip with a 6502 core) are all correct.

Why it’s interesting: Its low price helped start the home-computer boom.

31The Apollo Guidance Computer that flew to the Moon was built almost entirely from one kind of IC. Which logic gate?Hard

Answer: The 3-input NOR gate. The Block II computer used about 2,800 ICs, most of them holding two 3-input NOR gates.

Why it’s interesting: NOR is a universal gate, so any logic can be made from it. Using one chip type made testing and qualifying for spaceflight much easier.

32Nvidia’s Blackwell B200 GPU (2024) has 208 billion transistors. About how many Intel 4004s is that?Medium

Answer: About 90 million. 208,000,000,000 ÷ 2,300 ≈ 90.4 million.

Why it’s interesting: Even the B200 is two dies joined in one package, because a single die cannot grow beyond the maximum area a lithography tool can expose at once.

Round 5: Communication and radio

From sparks to 5G.

33James Clerk Maxwell’s 1865 equations predicted that electromagnetic waves travel at what speed?Medium

Answer: The speed of light. He concluded that light itself is an electromagnetic wave.

Why it’s interesting: Every antenna, waveguide and transmission line you study is a solution of Maxwell’s equations.

34Who first proved, in the late 1880s, that electromagnetic waves exist?Easy

Answer: Heinrich Hertz, using a spark-gap transmitter and a loop receiver.

Why it’s interesting: The unit of frequency, the hertz, is named after him.

35On 12 December 1901 Marconi’s team received the first transatlantic radio signal. What was the message?Medium

Answer: The Morse code letter “S” (three dots), sent from Poldhu in Cornwall and reported heard by Marconi at Signal Hill in Newfoundland.

Why it’s interesting: Many scientists believed radio waves could not follow the curve of the Earth. They were bent back by the ionosphere, which was not yet known about.

36Claude Shannon’s 1948 paper “A Mathematical Theory of Communication” put the word “bit” into print. Who did Shannon credit with coining it?Medium

Answer: John Tukey, a Bell Labs colleague. “Bit” is short for binary digit.

Why it’s interesting: The same paper defined channel capacity, the limit you meet as C = B log₂(1 + S/N) in communication theory.

37Who made the first public handheld mobile phone call, on 3 April 1973?Easy

Answer: Martin Cooper of Motorola, on a DynaTAC prototype. He called Joel Engel at Bell Labs, a rival company.

Why it’s interesting: The prototype weighed over a kilogram, and its battery gave around 30 minutes of talk time.

38Bluetooth is named after whom?Easy

Answer: Harald “Bluetooth” Gormsson, a 10th-century king of Denmark and Norway who united Danish tribes.

Why it’s interesting: The logo combines the Nordic runes for his initials, H (ᚼ) and B (ᛒ). The idea was a technology to unite devices.

39True or false: “Wi-Fi” stands for “Wireless Fidelity”.Hard

Answer: False. It is a brand name created by the branding firm Interbrand in 1999. It does not stand for anything.

Why it’s interesting: The technology itself is the IEEE 802.11 family of standards, first published in 1997.

40Sputnik 1 (4 October 1957) beeped on two frequencies so radio amateurs worldwide could hear it. Roughly what were they?Hard

Answer: About 20 MHz and 40 MHz (20.005 and 40.002 MHz).

Why it’s interesting: Scientists used the signals to study the ionosphere, and the Doppler shift of the beeps to work out the orbit. Tracking a satellite by Doppler later led to the US Transit navigation system, a forerunner of GPS.

Round 6: India in electronics

Home ground. You should get most of these.

41In 1895 in Calcutta, J. C. Bose demonstrated wireless signals at wavelengths as short as 5 mm. What frequency is that?Medium

Answer: About 60 GHz, in the millimetre-wave band.

Why it’s interesting: IEEE recognised his work as a Milestone in 2012. He also built early horn antennas, dielectric lenses and polarisers, parts now used in 5G and radar.

42Which Indian city is home to the Semi-Conductor Laboratory (SCL), India’s government chip fab?Medium

Answer: Mohali, Punjab.

Why it’s interesting: It was set up as Semiconductor Complex Limited, approved in 1976, and started production in 1984 with a 5 µm CMOS process. A fire in 1989 destroyed much of the plant. It was renamed Semi-Conductor Laboratory in 2006 and is under the Department of Space.

43Between whom was India’s first mobile phone call made, on 31 July 1995?Easy

Answer: West Bengal Chief Minister Jyoti Basu and Union Telecom Minister Sukh Ram. The call was made in Kolkata (then Calcutta) on the Modi Telstra GSM network.

Why it’s interesting: GSM is a 2G standard: it carried voice digitally, which also made SMS possible.

44The India Semiconductor Mission programme was approved by the Union Cabinet on 15 December 2021. What was its budget?Medium

Answer: ₹76,000 crore.

Why it’s interesting: It offers fiscal support of up to 50% of project cost for chip and display fabs, and 30% of capital cost for assembly and test (ATMP/OSAT) units.

45Where is India’s first commercial semiconductor fab being built, and by which partners?Medium

Answer: Dholera, Gujarat, by Tata Electronics with Powerchip Semiconductor Manufacturing Corporation (PSMC) of Taiwan.

Why it’s interesting: Approved on 29 February 2024, it has a planned investment of up to ₹91,000 crore and a capacity of up to 50,000 wafers a month, on nodes from 28 nm to 110 nm.

46Micron’s plant at Sanand, Gujarat, was inaugurated on 28 February 2026. Does it make memory wafers?Medium

Answer: No. It is an assembly and test (ATMP) facility: it receives finished DRAM and NAND wafers from Micron’s fabs abroad and turns them into packaged, tested memory products.

Why it’s interesting: Packaging and test are the “back end” of chipmaking. The total investment is about US$2.75 billion, shared by Micron and government partners.

47The Vikram 3201 processor, shown at Semicon India in September 2025, was developed by SCL. On what process node is it made, and how wide is it?Hard

Answer: It is a 32-bit processor made on SCL’s 180 nm process.

Why it’s interesting: It is built for space use and has flown on ISRO missions. It is named after Vikram Sarabhai, founder of India’s space programme.

48Keltron, one of India’s early public-sector electronics companies, was founded in 1973 in which state?Easy

Answer: Kerala. It was founded by K. P. P. Nambiar and is headquartered in Thiruvananthapuram.

Why it’s interesting: It was part of an early push to build an electronics industry in Kerala, and it is still a Government of Kerala company today.

Round 7: Units and symbols

Quick-fire. Speed matters in aptitude rounds too.

49What was the old unit of conductance, and why is it spelled that way?Easy

Answer: The mho (symbol ℧), which is “ohm” spelled backwards. The SI unit is the siemens (S).

Why it’s interesting: Conductance is 1/R, so its unit was written as the upside-down ohm. You will still see mho in older textbooks.

50Why do electrical engineers write j instead of i for √−1?Easy

Answer: Because i is already used for current.

Why it’s interesting: In phasor form, an inductor’s impedance is jωL and a capacitor’s is 1/(jωC).

51What power is 0 dBm, and what is 30 dBm?Medium

Answer: 0 dBm = 1 mW; 30 dBm = 1 W.

Why it’s interesting: Every +10 dB multiplies power by 10, and +3 dB roughly doubles it. A phone receiving −100 dBm is getting 0.1 picowatt.

52At the −3 dB cutoff frequency of a filter, what fraction of power and of voltage gets through?Medium

Answer: About half the power (0.501) and about 0.707 of the voltage (1/√2).

Why it’s interesting: That is why the cutoff frequency is also called the half-power point.

53The SI unit of inductance is named after which scientist?Easy

Answer: Joseph Henry, an American who discovered self-inductance around the same time as Faraday’s work on induction.

Why it’s interesting: One henry is large. The inductors in phone circuits are usually in nanohenries or microhenries.

54Since the SI redefinition of 20 May 2019, which constant has an exact value that defines the ampere?Hard

Answer: The elementary charge, e = 1.602176634 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, exactly.

Why it’s interesting: Before 2019 the ampere was defined by the force between two infinitely long parallel wires, which no lab could build exactly.

55In 2022 the SI added four new prefixes. Name the largest.Hard

Answer: Quetta (Q), 10³⁰. The others are ronna (10²⁷), ronto (10⁻²⁷) and quecto (10⁻³⁰).

Why it’s interesting: They were added mainly because the amount of data in the world was approaching the old limit, yotta (10²⁴).

56Why does conventional current flow from + to −, opposite to electron flow?Medium

Answer: Benjamin Franklin chose which charge to call “positive” in the 18th century, long before the electron was discovered in 1897. He guessed the wrong one to be the moving charge.

Why it’s interesting: Circuit analysis works the same either way, so the convention stayed. Only in device physics do you need to track electrons and holes separately.

Round 8: Weird and fun facts

The round you will tell your friends about.

57The first actual computer “bug” was a moth, found on 9 September 1947. In which computer?Easy

Answer: The Harvard Mark II, an electromechanical computer. The team taped the moth into the logbook with the note “First actual case of bug being found”.

Why it’s interesting: Engineers were already using “bug” to mean a fault (Edison used it in the 1870s). The joke was that this one was a real insect.

58About how many vacuum tubes did ENIAC (completed 1945) contain?Medium

Answer: About 17,468. It weighed around 27 tonnes and used about 150 kW of power.

Why it’s interesting: Tubes burned out regularly, so engineers ran them below their rated power to make them last longer. A single microcontroller today is far more powerful.

59Who coined the word “transistor”?Easy

Answer: John R. Pierce of Bell Labs, from “transfer” and “resistor” (the idea of a transresistance).

Why it’s interesting: Pierce was also a pioneer of satellite communication and wrote science fiction under a pen name.

60Why is flash memory called “flash”?Medium

Answer: Toshiba’s Shōji Ariizumi suggested the name because erasing a whole block at once reminded him of a camera flash. Fujio Masuoka invented it at Toshiba in 1980.

Why it’s interesting: Toshiba presented NOR flash in 1984 and NAND flash in 1987. NAND is now in every phone and SSD.

61Which metal used in GaN and GaAs chips melts if you hold it in your hand?Easy

Answer: Gallium. It melts at about 29.8 °C, below body temperature.

Why it’s interesting: GaAs is used in RF amplifiers and GaN in fast chargers and 5G base stations.

62Electrophorus voltai, an electric eel described in 2019, holds the record for the highest animal discharge. About how many volts?Medium

Answer: About 860 V.

Why it’s interesting: It stacks thousands of cells called electrocytes in series, like cells in a battery. The previous record was about 650 V.

63The world’s first webcam (1991, University of Cambridge) watched what?Medium

Answer: A coffee pot: the Trojan Room coffee pot, so people could see whether there was coffee before walking down the corridor.

Why it’s interesting: It was put on the World Wide Web in 1993 and became one of the web’s early attractions.

64Who popularised the name “Silicon Valley”, and in what year?Hard

Answer: Journalist Don Hoefler, in a series of articles in Electronic News starting in January 1971.

Why it’s interesting: The region grew from Fairchild Semiconductor, founded in 1957 by the “traitorous eight” who left Shockley Semiconductor. Intel and AMD were both started by people who had worked at Fairchild.

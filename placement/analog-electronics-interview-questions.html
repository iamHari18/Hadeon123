Diodes and rectifiers

Q1What happens at a PN junction when no voltage is applied?Easy

Electrons from the N side diffuse into the P side and holes diffuse the other way. They recombine near the junction and leave behind fixed ionised dopant atoms: positive donors on the N side, negative acceptors on the P side. This region has no free carriers, so it is called the depletion region. Its charge sets up an electric field and a built-in potential that stops further net diffusion.

For silicon the built-in potential is roughly 0.6–0.8 V depending on doping. You cannot measure it with a voltmeter across the diode, because the contact potentials at the metal terminals cancel it.

Q2Why is the forward drop of a silicon diode taken as about 0.7 V?Easy

Diode current rises exponentially: I = I_S (e^(V/(nV_T)) − 1), with VT ≈ 26 mV at room temperature. Below about 0.5 V the current is tiny; between 0.6 and 0.8 V it goes from microamps to tens of milliamps. 0.7 V is a convenient point inside that knee for typical mA-level currents. It is a model, not a constant: each tenfold rise in current adds about 60 mV (for n = 1).

Q3How does the diode forward voltage change with temperature?Medium

At a fixed current, VF falls by about 2 mV per °C. The saturation current IS roughly doubles every 5 °C or so, which outweighs the increase in VT. This negative temperature coefficient is why a diode or a VBE junction is used as a temperature sensor, and why bandgap references pair it with a PTAT voltage that rises with temperature.

Q5How do you size the filter capacitor for a full-wave rectifier?Medium

With a capacitor-input filter and load current IL, the capacitor discharges for roughly one ripple period between peaks. Peak-to-peak ripple is approximately

Vr(pp) ≈ IL / (fr · C)

where fr is the ripple frequency (2 × 50 = 100 Hz in India for full-wave). Example: 500 mA load, 1 V ripple allowed → C ≈ 0.5 / (100 × 1) = 5000 µF, so use a standard 4700 µF or 6800 µF part. A bigger C means smaller ripple but larger, shorter charging current pulses through the diodes and transformer.

Q6How does a Zener diode regulate voltage, and how do you pick the series resistor?Medium

In reverse breakdown, the Zener voltage stays nearly constant over a wide current range. A series resistor RS drops the difference between input and VZ; the Zener takes whatever current the load does not.

Pick RS so the Zener still has at least IZ(min) at the worst case (lowest input, highest load):

RS ≤ (Vin(min) − VZ) / (IL(max) + IZ(min))

Then check the other worst case (highest input, no load): all current flows in the Zener and P = VZ · IZ must stay under its rating. Below about 5 V breakdown is Zener (tunnelling) with a negative tempco; above about 6 V it is avalanche with a positive tempco. Diodes near 5–6 V have the lowest drift.

Q7What are clipper and clamper circuits?Easy

A clipper removes part of a waveform above or below a set level, using a diode plus a bias source; its output never exceeds that level. A clamper (DC restorer) shifts the whole waveform up or down without changing its shape, using a capacitor that charges to the peak through a diode. A positive clamper puts the negative peak at 0 V, so a 5 V peak sine ends up swinging 0 to 10 V. For clamping to work, the RC time constant must be much longer than the signal period.

BJT

Q8What are the operating regions of a BJT and what decides each?Easy

For an NPN transistor.

Cut-offReverseReverseSwitch off
Active (forward-active)ForwardReverseAmplifier

Reverse-activeReverseForwardRarely used; β is very low

In active mode IC = β IB. In saturation, IC is set by the external circuit and VCE(sat) is about 0.1–0.2 V.

Q9Define α and β and relate them.Easy

α = IC/IE (common-base current gain, slightly below 1, e.g. 0.99). β = IC/IB (common-emitter current gain, typically 50–300). Since IE = IC + IB:

β = α / (1 − α), α = β / (β + 1)

α = 0.99 gives β = 99. A small change in α causes a big change in β, which is why β varies so widely between devices of the same part number.

Q10What is the Early effect?Medium

In active mode, raising VCE widens the base-collector depletion region, which shrinks the effective base width. A narrower base means less recombination and a steeper carrier gradient, so IC rises slightly with VCE. If you extend the sloped output curves backwards they meet near VCE = −VA, the Early voltage (typically 50–200 V). It gives the transistor a finite output resistance ro = VA / IC, which limits the maximum gain of a stage.

Q11Why is voltage-divider bias preferred over fixed bias?Medium

In fixed bias, IB is set by one resistor and IC = β IB. β varies with temperature and between parts, so the operating point moves with it. Voltage-divider bias fixes the base voltage with a stiff divider, and an emitter resistor RE sets IE ≈ (VB − 0.7) / RE, which hardly depends on β. If IC tries to rise, VE rises, VBE falls and the current is pulled back: negative feedback. Rule of thumb: make the divider current about 10 × IB and VE at least 1 V.

Q12What is thermal runaway and how is it prevented?Medium

ICBO roughly doubles every 10 °C and VBE falls about 2 mV/°C. Both increase collector current, which increases power dissipation, which heats the junction further. If the heat cannot escape fast enough the loop runs away and destroys the transistor. Prevention: emitter degeneration (RE), stable bias networks, heat sinks, and keeping VCE at or below VCC/2 in class-A stages so that more current means less dissipation rather than more.

Q14Derive the small-signal voltage gain of a CE amplifier with an unbypassed emitter resistor.Hard

Using the hybrid-π model with gm = IC/VT and rπ = β/gm, ignoring ro:

Av = −β RC / (rπ + (β+1) RE) ≈ −RC / (re + RE)

where re = VT/IE ≈ 1/gm. If RE ≫ re, the gain becomes −RC/RE, independent of β and temperature. That is the trade: you give up gain to get a predictable, linear stage with higher input resistance. Bypassing RE with a capacitor restores full gain −gmRC at signal frequencies while keeping DC stability.

Q15What is the Miller effect?Medium

A capacitance C between the input and output of an inverting amplifier with gain −A looks, from the input, like a capacitance C(1 + A) to ground. In a CE stage, the small Cμ (base-collector) gets multiplied by the gain and dominates the input capacitance, which sets the upper −3 dB frequency. The cascode (CE driving a CB stage) fixes this by giving the first transistor a very low load, so its gain and Miller multiplication stay small.

MOSFET

Q16What are the regions of an n-channel enhancement MOSFET?Easy

Vov = VGS − Vth is the overdrive voltage.
RegionConditionDrain current (long-channel)
Cut-offVGS < VthID ≈ 0 (sub-threshold leakage only)
Triode / linearVGS > Vth, VDS < VovID = k′(W/L)[VovVDS − VDS2/2]
SaturationVGS > Vth, VDS ≥ VovID = ½ k′(W/L) Vov2 (1 + λVDS)

k′ = µnCox. Note that BJT "saturation" and MOSFET "saturation" mean opposite things: a MOSFET amplifier works in saturation, a BJT amplifier in active mode.

Q17What is pinch-off?Easy

When VDS reaches VGS − Vth, the gate-to-channel voltage at the drain end drops to Vth, so the inversion layer vanishes there. Current does not stop; carriers are swept across the small depleted region by the strong field. Beyond this point ID is roughly independent of VDS, which is the saturation region.

Q18Why does a MOSFET have near-infinite DC input resistance while a BJT does not?Medium

The MOSFET gate is insulated from the channel by a thin oxide, so no DC gate current flows (only tiny tunnelling leakage in very thin oxides). The BJT base is a forward-biased junction and must supply base current IC/β. At high frequency the MOSFET gate is not free to drive: its gate capacitance must be charged and discharged every cycle.

Q19Compare g_m of a MOSFET and a BJT at the same bias current.Medium

BJT: gm = IC / VT
MOSFET (saturation): gm = 2 ID / Vov = √(2 k′(W/L) ID)

At 1 mA, a BJT gives 1/26 mV ≈ 38 mS. A MOSFET with 200 mV overdrive gives 2 × 1 mA / 0.2 V = 10 mS. The BJT wins on gm per unit current because VT (26 mV) is smaller than any practical Vov/2. MOSFETs win on input resistance, density and ease of switching.

Q20What is channel-length modulation?Medium

In saturation, increasing VDS moves the pinch-off point towards the source, which shortens the effective channel length. Shorter L means more current, so ID rises slightly with VDS. It is modelled by (1 + λVDS) and gives output resistance ro ≈ 1 / (λ ID). λ is larger for short-channel devices, which is why minimum-length transistors give low intrinsic gain gmro.

Q21What is the body effect?Medium

If the source is above the body (substrate) potential, the depletion charge under the gate increases and the threshold voltage rises:

Vth = Vth0 + γ(√(2φF + VSB) − √(2φF))

It matters in stacked transistors (NAND gate pull-downs, source followers, pass transistors) where the source is not at ground. A source follower built in a common substrate therefore has gain below 1 even with an ideal current source load.

Q22Compare depletion-mode and enhancement-mode MOSFETs.Easy

An enhancement MOSFET has no channel at VGS = 0; it is normally off and needs VGS > Vth (positive for n-channel). A depletion MOSFET is built with a channel already present, so it is normally on and needs a negative VGS (n-channel) to turn off. Almost all CMOS logic uses enhancement devices because an off-by-default switch draws no static current.

Q23Why do power MOSFETs need gate drivers if the gate draws no DC current?Hard

A power MOSFET has gate charge Qg of tens of nanocoulombs. To switch in time t, the driver must supply I ≈ Qg/t: 50 nC in 50 ns needs 1 A peak. A microcontroller pin giving 20 mA would take 2.5 µs, and the device would spend that time in its linear region dissipating heavily. There is also the Miller plateau, where VGS stalls while Cgd is charged through the falling drain voltage. Gate drivers supply amp-level peak currents, level-shift for high-side switches, and add dead time in half-bridges.

Op-amps

Q24List the characteristics of an ideal op-amp.Easy

Infinite open-loop gain, infinite input impedance, zero output impedance, infinite bandwidth, zero offset voltage, infinite CMRR and slew rate, and zero noise. Two working rules follow when negative feedback is present: no current flows into the inputs, and the two input voltages are equal (virtual short).

Q25What is a virtual ground?Easy

In an inverting amplifier with the non-inverting input tied to ground, negative feedback forces the inverting input to the same potential, 0 V, even though it is not physically connected to ground. It is "virtual" because no current flows into it to ground: the input current through Rin flows on through Rf. That is what makes the gain −Rf/Rin and the input resistance exactly Rin.

Q27What is slew rate and how does it limit output?Medium

Slew rate is the maximum rate of change of output voltage, in V/µs, set by how fast internal currents can charge the compensation capacitor. For a sine of peak Vp, the maximum slope is 2πfVp, so the full-power bandwidth is

fmax = SR / (2π Vp)

A 741 (SR ≈ 0.5 V/µs) delivering a 10 V peak sine is limited to about 8 kHz before the output turns into a triangle. Slew limiting is a large-signal effect; bandwidth (GBW) is a small-signal effect.

Q28What is the gain-bandwidth product and how is it used?Medium

An internally compensated op-amp has a single dominant pole, so open-loop gain falls at −20 dB/decade. Along that slope, gain × frequency is constant, the GBW (also called unity-gain frequency fT). For a closed-loop non-inverting gain G (the noise gain), the −3 dB bandwidth is about GBW / G. With a 1 MHz op-amp, a gain-of-100 stage has only about 10 kHz bandwidth. If you need gain 100 at 50 kHz, use two stages of gain 10 or pick a faster part.

Q29Define CMRR and explain why it matters.Medium

CMRR = Ad/Acm, usually in dB (20 log). It measures how well the amplifier rejects a voltage common to both inputs, such as 50 Hz hum picked up equally on both wires of a sensor cable. In a four-resistor difference amplifier, resistor mismatch usually limits CMRR more than the op-amp itself: with 1 % resistors in a unity-gain stage the worst case is only about 34 dB. Instrumentation amplifiers and laser-trimmed networks are used when this matters.

Q30What are input offset voltage and input bias current, and how do you compensate them?Medium

Offset voltage Vos is the differential input needed to bring the output to zero; it appears at the output multiplied by the noise gain (1 + Rf/R1). Bias current IB is the DC current drawn by each input; flowing through unequal source resistances it creates an extra offset. The classic fix for bias current is a resistor at the non-inverting input equal to R1 ∥ Rf, so both inputs see the same DC resistance. What remains is the offset current Ios (the difference between the two bias currents).

Q31Why does an op-amp integrator need a resistor across its capacitor in practice?Hard

An ideal integrator has gain −1/(sRC), which is infinite at DC. Any input offset or bias current is integrated continuously, so the output drifts into a supply rail even with no input signal. A large resistor Rf across C limits DC gain to −Rf/R, turning it into a lossy integrator (a low-pass filter) that still integrates for frequencies well above 1/(2πRfC). The same problem in reverse affects differentiators: their gain rises with frequency, so they amplify noise and can oscillate unless a small series resistor limits high-frequency gain.

Q32How does a comparator differ from an op-amp, and why add hysteresis?Medium

A comparator is designed to run open-loop: it switches fast, recovers quickly from saturation and often has an open-collector or logic-level output. Op-amps are compensated for stable closed-loop use and are slow when used as comparators. With a noisy input crossing the threshold slowly, a plain comparator chatters between states. A Schmitt trigger adds positive feedback, creating two thresholds (upper and lower). The input must cross the full hysteresis gap to flip the output again, which ignores noise smaller than the gap.

Feedback

Q33Name the four feedback topologies and what each does to input and output impedance.Easy

Topology (sampling–mixing)Amplifier typeInput impedanceOutput impedance
Voltage–series (series–shunt)Voltage amplifierIncreasesDecreases
Voltage–shunt (shunt–shunt)TransresistanceDecreasesDecreases
Current–series (series–series)TransconductanceIncreasesIncreases
Current–shunt (shunt–series)Current amplifierDecreasesIncreases

Rule: series mixing raises input impedance, shunt mixing lowers it. Voltage sampling lowers output impedance, current sampling raises it. Each change is by the factor (1 + Aβ).

Q34What are the advantages of negative feedback?Easy

With loop gain Aβ ≫ 1, closed-loop gain is A/(1 + Aβ) ≈ 1/β, which depends only on passive components. Benefits: stable, predictable gain; less distortion; wider bandwidth (gain-bandwidth stays roughly constant); controllable input and output impedances; less sensitivity to device variation and temperature. The price is lower gain and a risk of instability if phase shift reaches 180° while loop gain is still ≥ 1.

Q35What are gain margin and phase margin?Medium

Both describe how far a feedback loop is from oscillation. Phase margin is 180° minus the loop's phase lag at the frequency where |Aβ| = 1. Gain margin is how far |Aβ| is below 1 (in dB) at the frequency where the phase lag is 180°. Typical targets: phase margin 45°–60°, gain margin above 10 dB. A phase margin of 45° shows about 23 % overshoot in a second-order step response; 60° gives about 9–10 %.

Q36Why are op-amps internally compensated, and what is pole splitting?Hard

An uncompensated multi-stage op-amp has several poles. At unity-feedback (β = 1), loop gain is highest, and the combined phase shift reaches 180° before gain falls to 1, so it oscillates. A small capacitor Cc is placed across the high-gain second stage. Through the Miller effect it creates a low-frequency dominant pole, and it also pushes the output pole to a higher frequency: pole splitting. The result is a single −20 dB/decade roll-off down to unity gain and a stable follower, at the cost of bandwidth and slew rate.

Oscillators

Q37State the Barkhausen criteria.Easy

For sustained oscillation at frequency f0, the loop gain must satisfy |Aβ| = 1 and total phase shift around the loop = 0° (or 360°). In practice the gain is set slightly above 1 at start-up so that noise builds up, and a non-linearity (amplitude limiting, an AGC element, or a lamp/thermistor/JFET in the feedback) brings it back to exactly 1 at steady state. The criteria are necessary but not sufficient for a stable oscillator.

Q38Give the frequency and gain condition of the RC phase-shift and Wien bridge oscillators.Medium

OscillatorFrequencyGain needed

In the phase-shift oscillator the RC ladder gives 180° and the inverting amplifier gives the other 180°. In the Wien bridge the RC network has 0° shift and 1/3 attenuation at f0, so a non-inverting gain of 3 completes the loop.

Q39Compare Colpitts and Hartley oscillators.Medium

Both are LC oscillators using a tapped tank. Colpitts uses a capacitive divider (C1, C2) with one inductor; Hartley uses a tapped inductor (L1, L2) with one capacitor.

Colpitts: f = 1 / (2π√(L·Ceq)), Ceq = C1C2/(C1+C2)
Hartley: f = 1 / (2π√((L1+L2+2M)C))

Colpitts is more common at RF because capacitors are cheaper and more precise than tapped inductors, and the capacitive divider shunts harmonics.

Q40Why are crystal oscillators so stable?Medium

A quartz crystal behaves as an extremely high-Q resonator (Q of 104 to 106) because of its piezoelectric mechanical resonance. Its reactance changes very steeply near resonance, so any phase disturbance in the amplifier shifts the frequency by only a tiny amount. Typical accuracy is ±10 to ±50 ppm, and temperature-compensated (TCXO) or oven-controlled (OCXO) types go much better. A crystal has a series resonance fs and a slightly higher parallel resonance fp; the oscillator circuit works between them where the crystal looks inductive.

Filters

Q41Compare passive and active filters.Easy

Passive filters use only R, L and C: no power supply, work at high frequency and high power, but inductors are bulky at low frequencies and there is no gain. Active filters add op-amps: they can give gain, have high input and low output impedance (so stages cascade without loading), and avoid inductors. Their limits are the op-amp bandwidth, supply range and noise.

Q42Compare Butterworth, Chebyshev and Bessel responses.Medium

Every filter type falls at 20n dB/decade far from cut-off, where n is the order. The difference is in the transition band and in how they treat time-domain signals. Use Bessel for pulses and data, Butterworth as the general default, Chebyshev when a sharp edge matters more than phase.

Q43What is the cut-off frequency and roll-off of a first-order RC low-pass filter?Medium

H(jω) = 1 / (1 + jωRC), fc = 1 / (2πRC)

At fc the gain is 1/√2 (−3 dB) and the phase is −45°. Beyond fc the gain falls at −20 dB/decade (−6 dB/octave). Example: R = 1.6 kΩ, C = 100 nF gives fc ≈ 995 Hz. A second-order filter falls at −40 dB/decade.

Q44Design a unity-gain Sallen-Key low-pass filter. What sets Q?Hard

For the unity-gain Sallen-Key low-pass with resistors R1, R2 and capacitors C1 (feedback, to output) and C2 (to ground):

ω0 = 1 / √(R1R2C1C2)
Q = √(R1R2C1C2) / (C2(R1+R2))

With equal resistors R, Q = ½√(C1/C2). For Butterworth (Q = 0.707) you need C1 = 2C2. Example: R = 10 kΩ, C1 = 22 nF, C2 = 11 nF gives f0 ≈ 1.02 kHz. Unity-gain Sallen-Key is popular because Q depends on component ratios, not on precise op-amp gain.

Network theory basics

Q45State Thevenin's and Norton's theorems.Easy

Any linear two-terminal network can be replaced by a voltage source Vth in series with Rth (Thevenin) or a current source IN in parallel with RN (Norton). Vth is the open-circuit voltage, IN is the short-circuit current, and Rth = RN = Vth/IN. To find Rth directly, switch off independent sources (voltage sources shorted, current sources opened) and look into the terminals. With dependent sources, keep them active and apply a test source.

Q46State the maximum power transfer theorem.Easy

A source with internal resistance Rs delivers the most power to a resistive load when RL = Rs. The maximum power is Vth2 / (4Rth), and efficiency at that point is only 50 %. For AC sources with complex impedance, the load must be the complex conjugate: ZL = Zs*. Power systems deliberately do not match (they want efficiency); RF and communication systems do match (they want power and no reflections).

Q47What is the time constant of an RC circuit, and how much does it charge in 1τ and 5τ?Medium

τ = RC for RC circuits and L/R for RL circuits. Charging from 0 to V:

v(t) = V(1 − e−t/τ)

1τ63.2 %

5τ is taken as "fully charged" in practice. 10 kΩ with 1 µF gives τ = 10 ms.

Q48What is resonance in a series RLC circuit and what is Q?Medium

At f0 = 1/(2π√(LC)), XL = XC, the reactances cancel, impedance is minimum (= R) and current is maximum and in phase with voltage. Quality factor:

Q = ω0L / R = 1/(ω0CR) = (1/R)√(L/C), bandwidth = f0 / Q

At resonance the voltage across L (or C) is Q times the source voltage, which can be dangerous in high-Q circuits. In a parallel RLC tank, impedance is maximum at resonance instead.

Q49When can you apply superposition, and when can't you?Medium

Superposition works for any linear network with multiple independent sources: find the response to each source alone (others switched off) and add. It applies to voltages and currents, but not to power, because power is proportional to the square of current: (I1+I2)2 ≠ I12+I22. Dependent sources are never switched off. It also does not apply to circuits with diodes or other non-linear elements unless you have linearised them around an operating point.

How to prepare

Analog rounds rarely test memory alone. Interviewers draw a circuit and ask you to bias it, find a gain, or say what happens if a component changes. Prepare in this order:

  1. Small-signal models first. Be able to draw the hybrid-π for BJT and MOSFET from memory and write gm, rπ, ro. Most "find the gain" questions reduce to this.
  2. Op-amp circuits by inspection. Practise writing the transfer function of inverting, non-inverting, summing, integrator, differentiator and difference amplifier in under a minute each.
  3. One numeric example per topic. Size a Zener resistor, a filter capacitor, a Sallen-Key filter. Numbers are where answers go wrong.
  4. Know the "why" behind each fix. Emitter degeneration, compensation capacitors, bypass capacitors, hysteresis — interviewers follow up with "why does that help?".
  5. Revise network theory. Thevenin, transients and resonance appear in almost every written test for core roles.
